This paper examines the effectiveness of Automated Writing Evaluation (AWE) tools in improving EFL/ESL writing quality and the role of teachers alongside these systems. A synthesis of literature and empirical data from Li et al. (2015) (Criterion, 70 students), Wang et al. (2020) (eRevise, 143 students), and Chen and Cheng (2008) (My Access!, 68 students) reveals that AWE enhances writing accuracy, learner autonomy, and motivation while reducing teacher workload. However, significant limitations persist. Students and instructors reported dissatisfaction with feedback on content and organization, and systems like Criterion demonstrated error flagging accuracy below 50%. Furthermore, unfocused feedback risks cognitively overloading low-proficiency learners, while the absence of dialogic interaction and sociocultural context hinders deep learning. Methodological constraints, including a lack of control groups and short-term assessments, prevent firm conclusions regarding long-term outcome transferability. Consequently, integrating human and automated feedback is essential, necessitating further rigorous longitudinal studies to validate pedagogical claims.
