The article addresses the lack of a stable theory for automatic differentiation (AD) on nonsmooth, nonconvex loss functions, where different programmatic implementations of the same mathematical function yield different numerical derivatives, creating spurious critical points. The authors introduce a class of elementary (log-exp) functions and their elementary selections (S) to model most deep learning objectives. A canonical surjection F between programs and functions is established, showing that AD operates on program representations rather than on functions mathematically. A selection derivative is defined via a fixed branch, and a set-valued operator Df is introduced as the convex hull of active branch gradients to connect with conservative fields and the Clarke subdifferential. The results prove that no nontrivial differential operator on functions satisfying basic AD requirements exists (Theorem 1), while AD on numerical code returns a selection derivative (Theorem 2). Df is shown to be a conservative field, and the set of artificial critical points has dimension strictly less than the ambient space, contains no local minima, and possesses a finite number of critical values (Theorems 3, 4, and 7; Proposition 8). It is proven that for randomly initialized mini-batch stochastic gradient methods under typical conditions, trajectories converge to Clarke critical points, and the impact of artificial critical points on training asymptotics is negligible (Theorem 4). The work provides an elementary mathematical framework that formalizes AD as implemented in modern libraries (TensorFlow, PyTorch), justifying its use beyond its original smooth domain as harmless for stochastic optimization.
