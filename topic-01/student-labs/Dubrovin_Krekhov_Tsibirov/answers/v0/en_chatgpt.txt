Large language models (LLMs) offer potential for scientific summarisation but may generate hallucinations when summaries are inconsistent with their source material. This pilot study investigates whether prompt engineering can mitigate context inconsistency hallucinations in zero-shot scientific summarisation by improving the lexical and semantic alignment of generated summaries with source abstracts. Eight open-access yeast biotechnology abstracts were summarised using six instruction-tuned LLMs under seven prompt conditions: a baseline prompt, two levels of increasing instruction complexity, and four methods involving the repetition of semantically relevant or randomly selected sentences. In total, 336 summaries were generated and evaluated using six automatic metrics: ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L, BERTScore, METEOR, and cosine similarity. Statistical significance was assessed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni–Holm correction and bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap confidence intervals. The results demonstrate that repeating key sentences significantly improved lexical alignment with the source abstracts, while random sentence addition produced the strongest improvements in lexical alignment and also improved semantic alignment in several experimental conditions. In contrast, increasing instruction complexity did not consistently improve alignment, and the simplest engineered prompt significantly reduced semantic alignment. Repetition of key sentences produced only limited improvements when the repeated sentences themselves served as the evaluation reference. These findings suggest that incorporating additional source text into prompts may be more effective than increasing instruction complexity for improving alignment in zero-shot scientific summarisation. However, the small corpus and reliance on automatic evaluation metrics limit the generalisability of the findings. This pilot study provides preliminary evidence that simple prompt modifications may help mitigate context inconsistency hallucinations in LLM-generated scientific summaries, motivating further investigation across larger and more diverse scientific datasets.
