Large language models (LLMs) often hallucinate, and in scientific summarisation that can distort knowledge and erode trust in scientific information. This pilot study tests whether prompt design can reduce context inconsistency hallucinations, a type of faithfulness hallucination, in zero-shot summarisation of scientific abstracts. Abstracts from eight open-access yeast biotechnology papers were summarised by six instruction-tuned LLMs (7B to 72B parameters) under seven prompt conditions. The conditions were a minimal baseline prompt, two prompts with increasing instruction complexity (PE-1 and PE-2), context repetition of one or two semantically key sentences chosen by embedding similarity (CR-K1 and CR-K2), and random addition of one or two abstract sentences (RA-K1 and RA-K2). This produced 336 summaries. Hallucination was measured by proxy as the lexical and semantic alignment between each summary and its source abstract, and also its alignment with the repeated key sentences. Six metrics were used: ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L, BERTScore, METEOR and cosine similarity. Each prompt method was compared with the baseline using paired differences. Significance was tested with Holm-Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and BCa bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, and only effects significant under both tests were accepted. Context repetition significantly improved lexical alignment with the abstract on all ROUGE metrics, and random addition gave the largest gains in both lexical and semantic alignment. Greater instruction complexity did not improve semantic alignment. PE-1 significantly reduced BERTScore, METEOR and cosine similarity, and PE-2 had no significant effect. Context repetition increased alignment with the repeated key sentences only on ROUGE-2 for K = 2. These results suggest that repeating source content in the prompt is a simple, low-cost way to improve the faithfulness of LLM-generated scientific summaries, while extra instructions can be counterproductive. Because random sentences outperformed semantically selected ones, prompt length and repetition itself, rather than semantic relevance, may drive the effect. The main limitations are the small corpus, the lack of human validation and possible length confounds.
