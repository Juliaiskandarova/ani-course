Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to summarise scientific literature, but they can produce context inconsistency hallucinations, where summaries are unfaithful to the provided source text. This pilot study investigates whether prompt engineering can mitigate such hallucinations in zero-shot summarisation of scientific abstracts. Eight open-access yeast biotechnology abstracts were summarised by six instruction-tuned LLMs (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-Nemotron-70B-Instruct-HF) under seven prompt methods, giving 336 summaries. The methods were a minimal baseline prompt, two prompts of increasing instruction complexity (PE-1, PE-2), context repetition of one or two key sentences selected by embedding similarity (CR-K1, CR-K2), and a control that repeated one or two randomly chosen abstract sentences (RA-K1, RA-K2). Alignment between summaries and the reference text was used as a proxy for hallucination and was measured with ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L, BERTScore, METEOR and cosine similarity. Differences from the baseline were tested using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni-Holm correction and BCa bootstrap confidence intervals, and only results significant under both tests were retained. Context repetition significantly improved lexical alignment with the abstract. Random sentence addition improved lexical alignment as well, and by more than context repetition, so the gain cannot be attributed to the semantic relevance of the repeated content. Increased instruction complexity did not improve semantic alignment: PE-1 significantly reduced it and PE-2 had no significant effect. Context repetition increased alignment with the repeated key sentences only for K = 2 and only under ROUGE-2. These findings suggest that simple prompt modifications can affect summary alignment, but the small corpus, the use of automatic metrics without human validation and the zero-shot setting limit generalisation, and further work is needed to separate the effects of repetition, prompt length and sentence relevance.
