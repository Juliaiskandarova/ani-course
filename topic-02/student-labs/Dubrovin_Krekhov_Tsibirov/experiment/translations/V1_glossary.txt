PART A
Title: Method of Convolutional Gradient Descent
Abstract: The paper examines whether applying a sliding convolutional filter to the computed gradient can improve gradient descent. The approach addresses objective functions with challenging landscapes. The computational procedure is also interpreted as a neural network structure. According to the study, numerical experiments confirmed the accuracy and efficiency of the proposed method.
Keywords: global optimization, objective function, gradient method, nonlinear function, neural network, algorithm.

PART B
Gradient descent is a numerical optimization method used to locate the minimum of an objective function. At each iteration, the algorithm computes the gradient of the loss function and updates the model parameters in the opposite direction. The magnitude of the update depends on the learning rate: an overly large step may produce unstable behavior, whereas a very small one slows convergence. For a nonlinear function with a complicated landscape, the standard procedure can struggle to construct a stable sequence of updates.

In the approach examined here, a sliding convolutional filter is applied instead of using the computed gradient directly. The filter processes neighboring entries in a sequence and produces a modified estimate of the parameter-update direction. This may reduce the influence of abrupt variations, although the result depends on filter settings and the problem. Convolution must be distinguished from simple averaging: the filter coefficients determine how the inputs are combined.

The algorithm also allows a neural network interpretation. The parameter vector enters a gradient computation block; the output is then passed to a one-dimensional convolutional layer, after which the next approximation is obtained. This computational structure makes it possible to view the sequence of operations as a neural network without conflating the conventional gradient method with the proposed modification. Numerical experiments on several functions are needed to assess the method. Such an assessment should consider solution accuracy, convergence rate, and stability as the step size varies. A comparison with classical gradient descent is meaningful when the initial conditions, stopping criteria, and computing resources are identical. Under these conditions, an improvement can be attributed to the filter rather than experimental settings.
