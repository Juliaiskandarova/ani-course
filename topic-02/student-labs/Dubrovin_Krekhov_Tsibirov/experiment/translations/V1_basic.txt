PART A
Title: Method of Convolution Gradient Descent
Abstract: The paper explores a way to make gradient descent work better by passing the gradient through a moving convolution filter. It is meant for target functions with difficult landscapes. The author presents the calculation as a neural net structure. Numerical trials indicate that the proposed algorithm can deliver accurate answers with good efficiency.
Keywords: global optimization, target function, gradient technique, nonlinear function, neural net, algorithm.

PART B
Gradient descent belongs to numerical optimization methods and is used to find the minimum of a purpose function. At the next iteration, the algorithm calculates the gradient of the error function and changes the model parameters in the direction opposite to the gradient. The amount of change depends on the learning speed: an excessively large step can cause instability, while a very small one slows coming together. For a nonlinear function with a complex landscape, the usual scheme sometimes has trouble creating a stable series of updates.

In the approach under study, a moving convolution filter is applied instead of directly using the calculated gradient. It processes neighboring values in a sequence and produces a modified estimate of the direction of parameter adjustment. This operation may reduce the effect of sharp fluctuations, although the result depends on filter settings and the problem. Convolution should not be confused with simple averaging: the filter coefficients determine how the inputs are combined.

The algorithm can be interpreted as a neural net. A parameter vector enters a gradient-computing block; the result then passes to a one-dimensional convolutional layer, and the next approximation is formed. This scheme represents the sequence of operations as a neural net without equating the ordinary gradient method with the proposed modification. Numerical experiments with several functions are required to test the behavior of the method. The analysis should consider solution accuracy, the speed of coming together, and stability when the step changes. Comparison with classical gradient descent is meaningful only under the same initial conditions, stopping rules, and computational resources. Only then can improved performance be attributed to filtering rather than experimental settings.
